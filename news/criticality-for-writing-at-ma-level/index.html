Criticality for Writing at MA Level

When writing your first essay as a postgraduate student, you might come up against comments like "Your writing is mostly descriptive. Should be more critical." That's when you wonder, "What do they mean by 'being critical'?" Does it mean criticising other people's work the whole time, or does it mean something else?

Firstly, the adjective "critical," deriving from "criticise," may carry very aggressive connotations in languages such as Vietnamese; and in general discourse, it oftentimes means "finding mistakes, shortcomings, or wrongdoings of somebody and explicitly stating them out." In academic writing, especially at the postgraduate level, it simply does not mean this.

The word "criticise" basically means making a careful and sophisticated judgement about something. Being critical in your own essays means you evaluate other people's work and draw a meaningful connection between it and your own, showing alignment and/or contradiction.

Also, judgement in the academic world doesn't come (only) from personal opinions. It's true that as human beings, we have our own subjectivity—or epistemic stance, as a fancier term—but we don't simply say "I think ..." Instead, we support our judgement with evidence, mostly in the form of collected data or from the results of other people's research.

So, the easy steps and tips for beginners in the MA journey are:

  1. Form a stance or an argument. Don't ever fear being criticised—it's the fabric of academia. Good science should be defensive, meaning scientific research should anticipate criticism, test its own limits, and actively try to prove itself wrong.

  2. Evaluate other people's work in the same or relevant topics as yours. Here's where you should be critical. When you know your topic and your argument well, you'll know how other people's research aligns with or contradicts yours. There is no right or wrong—there's just good justification. Know your context and your questions.

  3. Build a coherent outline for your argument. Many people tend to ignore this step when they talk about criticality. But being critical also means arranging your ideas in a logical way so that your argument is at its strongest when presented. Choose one single main argument and see what ideas can support it. Wisely arrange evidence as scaffolding for your argument, not speaking on behalf of it.

  4. Don't just cite people; try to explain what the cited work means. For example, don't just write, "McMillans (2025) argues that ..." Add one or two sentences explaining why his argument matters and/or how it is relevant to your own argument.

Remember: being critical means evaluating people's work for its relevance to your own. It includes careful evaluation and sophisticated justification.
